\question \textbf{Exercise 1.11.}

Let $Z\sim N(\mu,1)$, where $\mu\in\R$.

\begin{parts}

\part For $\lambda\geq0$, define the ridge estimator
\[
\widehat\mu_\lambda(z)
=
\argmin_{t\in\R}
\left\{
\frac12(t-z)^2+\frac{\lambda}{2}t^2
\right\}.
\]
Compute
\[
\MSE_\lambda(\mu)
=
\E\{(\widehat\mu_\lambda(Z)-\mu)^2\}.
\]
Plot and compare the risk functions for
$\lambda=0.1,1,5,10$.

\part Repeat for the Lasso estimator
\[
\widehat\mu_\lambda(z)
=
\argmin_{t\in\R}
\left\{
\frac12(t-z)^2+\lambda|t|
\right\}.
\]

\end{parts}

\begin{solution}

\textbf{(a) Ridge.}
The derivative of the objective is
\[
(t-z)+\lambda t=(1+\lambda)t-z.
\]
Its second derivative is $1+\lambda>0$, so the unique minimizer is
\[
\boxed{
\widehat\mu_\lambda(z)=\frac{z}{1+\lambda}.
}
\]

Since $\E Z=\mu$ and $\Var(Z)=1$,
\[
\E\widehat\mu_\lambda(Z)-\mu
=
-\frac{\lambda\mu}{1+\lambda},
\qquad
\Var\{\widehat\mu_\lambda(Z)\}
=
\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)^2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{ridge}}(\mu)
=
\frac{1+\lambda^2\mu^2}{(1+\lambda)^2}.
}
\]

Each risk curve is even in $\mu$ and has its minimum at zero.
Larger penalties reduce variance near zero but increase shrinkage
bias for sufficiently large signals.

The unpenalized estimator $Z$ has MSE 1. For $\lambda>0$,
\[
\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{ridge}}(\mu)<1
\iff
1+\lambda^2\mu^2<(1+\lambda)^2
\iff
\boxed{
\mu^2<1+\frac{2}{\lambda}.
}
\]
Thus a positive ridge penalty improves on $Z$ exactly on a bounded
interval of signal sizes.

At $\mu=0$, the risks for the four penalties are approximately
\[
0.826446,\qquad
0.25,\qquad
0.027778,\qquad
0.008264.
\]

\textbf{(b) Lasso.}
The objective is the sum of the strictly convex function
$\frac12(t-z)^2$ and the convex function $\lambda|t|$, so it is
strictly convex and has a unique minimizer, characterized by
\[
0\in t-z+\lambda\partial|t|.
\]
Considering positive, zero, and negative $t$ gives
\[
\boxed{
\widehat\mu_\lambda(z)
=
S_\lambda(z)
=
\begin{cases}
z-\lambda, & z>\lambda,\\
0, & |z|\leq\lambda,\\
z+\lambda, & z<-\lambda.
\end{cases}
}
\]

Write $Z=\mu+U$, where $U\sim N(0,1)$, and define
\[
a=-\lambda-\mu,
\qquad
b=\lambda-\mu.
\]
Then
\[
\widehat\mu_\lambda(Z)-\mu
=
\begin{cases}
U+\lambda, & U<a,\\
-\mu, & a\leq U\leq b,\\
U-\lambda, & U>b.
\end{cases}
\]

Let $\phi$ and $\Phi$ denote the standard normal density and CDF.
The MSE is
\begin{align*}
\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{lasso}}(\mu)
={}&
\int_{-\infty}^{a}(u+\lambda)^2\phi(u)\,du\\
&+\mu^2\{\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)\}\\
&+\int_b^\infty(u-\lambda)^2\phi(u)\,du.
\end{align*}

Using $\phi'(u)=-u\phi(u)$ and integration by parts,
\begin{align*}
\int_{-\infty}^{a}u\phi(u)\,du
&=-\phi(a),\\
\int_{-\infty}^{a}u^2\phi(u)\,du
&=\Phi(a)-a\phi(a),\\
\int_b^\infty u\phi(u)\,du
&=\phi(b),\\
\int_b^\infty u^2\phi(u)\,du
&=1-\Phi(b)+b\phi(b).
\end{align*}

Expanding the squares and substituting yields
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{lasso}}(\mu)
={}&
(1+\lambda^2)\{\Phi(a)+1-\Phi(b)\}\\
&-(a+2\lambda)\phi(a)\\
&+(b-2\lambda)\phi(b)\\
&+\mu^2\{\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)\}.
\end{aligned}
}
\]

At $\lambda=0$, the formula reduces to 1.
At $\mu=0$, it becomes
\[
\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{lasso}}(0)
=
2\{(1+\lambda^2)\Phi(-\lambda)-\lambda\phi(\lambda)\}.
\]

The risk is even in $\mu$. Large penalties produce small risk near
zero by setting the estimator to zero with high probability.
For fixed $\lambda$,
\[
\boxed{
\lim_{|\mu|\to\infty}
\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{lasso}}(\mu)
=
1+\lambda^2.
}
\]
To justify this, note $|S_\lambda(z)-z|\le\lambda$ for all $z$, so
\[
\{S_\lambda(\mu+U)-\mu\}^2\le(|U|+\lambda)^2,
\]
which is integrable and does not depend on $\mu$. For each fixed $U$,
$\mu+U>\lambda$ once $\mu$ is large, so
$S_\lambda(\mu+U)-\mu\to U-\lambda$ pointwise as $\mu\to\infty$.
Dominated convergence gives
$\MSE_\lambda^{\mathrm{lasso}}(\mu)\to\E(U-\lambda)^2=1+\lambda^2$;
evenness handles $\mu\to-\infty$.
The limiting risks for the four penalties are
\[
1.01,\qquad 2,\qquad 26,\qquad 101.
\]
Unlike ridge, the Lasso risk approaches a finite plateau for each
fixed penalty.

\textbf{Comparison.}
Both estimators reduce risk near zero by shrinking toward it.
Ridge shrinks proportionally, so for $\lambda>0$ its bias
$-\lambda\mu/(1+\lambda)$ grows linearly in $|\mu|$ and its risk
diverges as $|\mu|\to\infty$.
Lasso subtracts at most $\lambda$, so its risk tends to the finite
limit $1+\lambda^2$.
Thus, for each fixed positive penalty, Lasso avoids the unbounded
large-signal risk of ridge. At $\mu=0$, ridge
has the smaller risk for $\lambda=0.1$ (0.826 versus 0.850), but
Lasso has the smaller risk for $\lambda=1,5,10$, because it sets the
estimate exactly to zero with high probability.
Both improve on the unpenalized estimator in a neighborhood of zero,
but for $\lambda>0$ neither dominates it uniformly: each has risk
exceeding 1 for all sufficiently large $|\mu|$.

\resultfigure{ex111_mse.pdf}{
Exact ridge and Lasso MSE curves for $\lambda=0.1,1,5,10$:
full range (top) and detail for $|\mu|\leq3$ (bottom); the detail
panels restrict the vertical range to emphasize differences near the
unpenalized benchmark, so some curves are clipped above 3.
The dashed line is the unpenalized risk 1.
}

\end{solution}

\question \textbf{Exercise 1.12.}

Let $Y_i$ be responses and let $X_i=X_{i,1:p}$ denote covariates
excluding a constant. Define
\[
\widetilde Y_i=Y_i-\overline Y,
\qquad
\widetilde X_i=X_i-\overline X.
\]

\begin{parts}

\part For ridge regression with an unpenalized intercept, show that
the slopes can be obtained from the centered data and that
\[
\widehat\beta_0
=
\overline Y-\overline X\trans\widetilde\beta_{1:p}.
\]
Also show that
\[
\tr(S_\lambda)=1+\tr(\widetilde S_\lambda).
\]

\part Prove the same centering and intercept identities for Lasso.

\end{parts}

\begin{solution}

\textbf{(a)}
Write $b=\beta_{1:p}$.
The ridge criterion is
\[
Q(\beta_0,b)
=
\frac{1}{2n}
\sum_{i=1}^n
(Y_i-\beta_0-X_i\trans b)^2
+
\frac{\lambda}{2}\normtwo{b}^2.
\]

For fixed $b$, the penalty is independent of the intercept.
Differentiating in $\beta_0$ gives
\[
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_0}
=
-\frac1n
\sum_{i=1}^n
(Y_i-\beta_0-X_i\trans b).
\]
The second derivative is 1, so the unique minimizing intercept is
\[
\beta_0(b)=\overline Y-\overline X\trans b.
\]

Substituting into the residual gives
\[
Y_i-\beta_0(b)-X_i\trans b
=
\widetilde Y_i-\widetilde X_i\trans b.
\]
Consequently the profiled objective is
\[
\min_{\beta_0}Q(\beta_0,b)
=
\frac{1}{2n}
\sum_{i=1}^n
(\widetilde Y_i-\widetilde X_i\trans b)^2
+
\frac{\lambda}{2}\normtwo{b}^2.
\]
This is exactly the centered ridge problem. Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\widehat\beta_{1:p}=\widetilde\beta_{1:p},
\qquad
\widehat\beta_0
=
\overline Y-\overline X\trans\widetilde\beta_{1:p}.
}
\]
More precisely, if $\widetilde b$ is any minimizer of the centered
problem, then $(\overline Y-\overline X\trans\widetilde b,\widetilde b)$
minimizes the original problem; conversely, every minimizer of the
original problem has this form, since its intercept must equal
$\beta_0(b)$. Thus the sets of slope minimizers coincide, and the
displayed equality is literal whenever the slope solution is unique
(for example, for every $\lambda>0$).

For the smoother identity, let $X$ be the original (uncentered)
$n\times p$ covariate matrix and define
\[
J=\frac1n\ones\ones\trans,
\qquad
H=I-J.
\]
Then
\[
\widetilde X=HX,
\qquad
\widetilde Y=HY.
\]

For $\lambda>0$, the centered smoother is
\[
\widetilde S_\lambda
=
\widetilde X
(\widetilde X\trans\widetilde X+n\lambda I)^{-1}
\widetilde X\trans.
\]
Because $\widetilde X\trans\ones=0$,
\[
\widetilde S_\lambda J=0,
\qquad
\widetilde S_\lambda H=\widetilde S_\lambda.
\]

The fitted response from the original regression is
\begin{align*}
\widehat Y
&=
\ones\widehat\beta_0+X\widetilde\beta_{1:p}\\
&=
\overline Y\ones+\widetilde X\widetilde\beta_{1:p}\\
&=
JY+\widetilde S_\lambda HY\\
&=
(J+\widetilde S_\lambda)Y.
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
S_\lambda=J+\widetilde S_\lambda.
\]
Since $\tr(J)=1$,
\[
\boxed{
\tr(S_\lambda)=1+\tr(\widetilde S_\lambda).
}
\]

At $\lambda=0$, use the centered least-squares projection matrix,
defined with a Moore--Penrose inverse when necessary.
The same fitted-value argument applies.

\textbf{(b)}
For Lasso, the objective becomes
\[
Q(\beta_0,b)
=
\frac{1}{2n}
\sum_{i=1}^n
(Y_i-\beta_0-X_i\trans b)^2
+
\lambda\|b\|_1.
\]
The penalty still does not involve $\beta_0$, so the optimal
intercept and residual substitution are unchanged, and the same
correspondence of minimizer sets holds. Hence
\[
\boxed{
\widehat\beta_{1:p}=\widetilde\beta_{1:p},
\qquad
\widehat\beta_0
=
\overline Y-\overline X\trans\widetilde\beta_{1:p}.
}
\]
No differentiation of the Lasso penalty is needed.

\end{solution}

\question \textbf{Exercise 1.13.}

Let
\[
s_j=\|X_j\|_n
=
\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_{ij}^2\right)^{1/2},
\qquad
\widetilde X_{ij}=\frac{X_{ij}}{s_j}.
\]

\begin{parts}

\part Find weights $c_j$ so that fitting an unweighted ridge penalty
to the standardized covariates and setting
$\widehat\beta_j=\widehat\gamma_j/s_j$ is equivalent to fitting
a weighted ridge penalty to the original covariates.

\part Find the corresponding weights for Lasso.

\end{parts}

\begin{solution}

Assume $s_j>0$ for each $j$, since otherwise the stated
standardization is undefined.

Let
\[
D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_p).
\]
Then $\widetilde X=XD^{-1}$, and the change of variables
\[
\gamma=D\beta
\]
is a bijection satisfying
\[
\widetilde X\gamma=X\beta.
\]

\textbf{(a) Ridge.}
The standardized objective becomes
\begin{align*}
&
\frac1{2n}\|Y-\widetilde X\gamma\|_2^2
+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^p\gamma_j^2\\
&\qquad=
\frac1{2n}\|Y-X\beta\|_2^2
+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^p s_j^2\beta_j^2.
\end{align*}
The objectives agree under the bijection, so their minimizers
correspond. Therefore
\[
\boxed{
c_j=s_j^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_{ij}^2.
}
\]

\textbf{(b) Lasso.}
Similarly,
\begin{align*}
&
\frac1{2n}\|Y-\widetilde X\gamma\|_2^2
+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^p|\gamma_j|\\
&\qquad=
\frac1{2n}\|Y-X\beta\|_2^2
+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^p s_j|\beta_j|.
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
\boxed{
c_j=s_j
=
\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_{ij}^2\right)^{1/2}.
}
\]

Standardization therefore changes the relative penalty weights
on the original coefficients: a predictor with larger empirical
scale $s_j$ receives a larger penalty on its original-scale
coefficient, quadratically in $s_j$ for ridge and linearly for Lasso.
These weights make the original-scale problem equivalent to
penalizing all standardized coefficients equally.
Here $s_j$ is the uncentered root mean square defined above, not
the centered sample standard deviation.

\end{solution}

\question \textbf{Exercise 1.15.}

\begin{parts}

\part For the Lasso objective
\[
h(\beta)
=
\frac1{2n}\|Y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1,
\]
show that coordinate-wise minimization implies joint minimization.

\part Generalize to
\[
h(\beta)=h_0(\beta)+\sum_{j=1}^p h_{1,j}(\beta_j),
\]
where $h_0$ is convex and everywhere differentiable, and each
$h_{1,j}$ is convex but may be nondifferentiable.

\part Give a convex counterexample for which
coordinate-wise minimization does not imply joint minimization.

\end{parts}

\begin{solution}

\textbf{(a)}
Let
\[
h_0(\beta)=\frac1{2n}\|Y-X\beta\|_2^2.
\]
Its gradient is
\[
\nabla h_0(\beta)
=
-\frac1nX\trans(Y-X\beta).
\]

Suppose $\widetilde\beta$ minimizes the objective in every
coordinate when the other coordinates are held fixed.
For each $j$ define the one-dimensional convex function
\[
g_j(t)=h_0(\widetilde\beta_1,\ldots,\widetilde\beta_{j-1},t,
\widetilde\beta_{j+1},\ldots,\widetilde\beta_p)+\lambda|t|
+\lambda\sum_{k\neq j}|\widetilde\beta_k|.
\]
Coordinate-wise optimality means $\widetilde\beta_j$ minimizes $g_j$,
so $0\in\partial g_j(\widetilde\beta_j)$. Since the first term is
differentiable, the subdifferential sum rule gives the
one-dimensional optimality condition
\[
0\in
\frac{\partial h_0}{\partial\beta_j}(\widetilde\beta)
+\lambda\partial|\widetilde\beta_j|.
\]

The absolute-value subdifferential is
\[
\partial|t|
=
\begin{cases}
\{1\}, & t>0,\\
{[-1,1]}, & t=0,\\
\{-1\}, & t<0.
\end{cases}
\]

For each $j$, select
$s_j\in\partial|\widetilde\beta_j|$ such that
\[
\frac{\partial h_0}{\partial\beta_j}(\widetilde\beta)
+\lambda s_j=0.
\]
Since the $L_1$ penalty is separable,
\[
s=(s_1,\ldots,s_p)\trans
\in\partial\|\widetilde\beta\|_1.
\]
Combining the coordinate conditions gives
\[
0
=
\nabla h_0(\widetilde\beta)+\lambda s
\in\partial h(\widetilde\beta).
\]

By the subgradient inequality, for every $\beta$,
\[
h(\beta)
\geq
h(\widetilde\beta)
+
0\trans(\beta-\widetilde\beta)
=
h(\widetilde\beta).
\]
Therefore $\widetilde\beta$ is a global minimizer.

This establishes the optimality of a coordinate-wise minimizing
point; it is not by itself a proof of convergence of every
coordinate-descent implementation.

\textbf{(b)}
Assume each $h_{1,j}$ is a finite-valued convex function on $\R$,
so that $\partial h_{1,j}(t)$ is nonempty for every $t$ and the
subdifferential sum rule applies to $g_j$ below.
As in part (a), apply $0\in\partial g_j(\widetilde\beta_j)$ to
$g_j(t)=h_0(\widetilde\beta_1,\ldots,t,\ldots,\widetilde\beta_p)+h_{1,j}(t)$
(plus constants); because $h_0$ is differentiable,
$\partial g_j(\widetilde\beta_j)=\partial_jh_0(\widetilde\beta)
+\partial h_{1,j}(\widetilde\beta_j)$.
Hence coordinate-wise optimality implies that, for each $j$, there exists
\[
s_j\in\partial h_{1,j}(\widetilde\beta_j)
\]
satisfying
\[
s_j=-\frac{\partial h_0}{\partial\beta_j}(\widetilde\beta).
\]

Convexity of $h_0$ gives
\[
h_0(\beta)
\geq
h_0(\widetilde\beta)
+
\nabla h_0(\widetilde\beta)\trans
(\beta-\widetilde\beta).
\]
The subgradient inequality for each penalty term gives
\[
h_{1,j}(\beta_j)
\geq
h_{1,j}(\widetilde\beta_j)
+
s_j(\beta_j-\widetilde\beta_j).
\]

Adding these inequalities,
\begin{align*}
h(\beta)
&\geq
h(\widetilde\beta)
+
\sum_{j=1}^p
\left\{
\frac{\partial h_0}{\partial\beta_j}(\widetilde\beta)
+s_j
\right\}
(\beta_j-\widetilde\beta_j)\\
&=
h(\widetilde\beta).
\end{align*}
Hence $\widetilde\beta$ is jointly minimizing.
Separability allows the individual penalty subgradients to
be chosen simultaneously.

\textbf{(c)}
Consider
\[
\boxed{
h(x,y)=|x-y|+\frac14(x+y-2)^2.
}
\]
The function is convex because it is the sum of the absolute
value of a linear function and the square of an affine function.

At $(0,0)$, fixing $y=0$ gives
\[
h(x,0)-h(0,0)
=
|x|-x+\frac{x^2}{4}
\geq0.
\]
Equality holds only at $x=0$.
By symmetry,
\[
h(0,y)-h(0,0)
=
|y|-y+\frac{y^2}{4}
\geq0,
\]
with equality only at $y=0$.

Thus $(0,0)$ uniquely minimizes the objective along each
coordinate line. However,
\[
h(0,0)=1,
\qquad
h(1,1)=0.
\]
Since $h\geq0$, $(1,1)$ is a global minimizer and $(0,0)$ is not.

The nondifferentiable term $|x-y|$ couples the coordinates;
it does not satisfy the separability assumption in part (b).

\end{solution}

\question \textbf{Exercise 1.17.}

Using the provided simulation code (seed 0), generate $n=200$
training and $n_{\rm test}=100$ test observations with $p=20$,
\[
X\sim N_p(0,\Sigma),\qquad \Sigma_{jk}=0.8^{|j-k|},
\]
\[
Y=X_1+\tfrac13\{\max(0,X_2+X_3+X_4)\}^2+\varepsilon,\qquad
\varepsilon\sim N(0,0.5^2).
\]
Apply all methods without standardizing $x$ or $y$.

\begin{parts}

\part Fit ridge regression with an unpenalized intercept and
produce a coefficient path.

\part Plot training, test, 5-fold CV, and 10-fold CV errors
against degrees of freedom. Report the minimum test error and
test errors at the minimum-CV and one-standard-error choices.

\part Fit Lasso with an unpenalized intercept and produce
a coefficient path.

\part Plot the four errors against the shrinkage fraction and
report the corresponding test-error comparisons.

\part Summarize the findings in one paragraph.

\end{parts}

\begin{solution}

\textbf{Setup.}
Write $m(X)=X_1+\frac13\{\max(0,X_2+X_3+X_4)\}^2$ for the
generating conditional mean.
All computations are done by \texttt{hw2.R}, submitted separately
(see the reproducibility note at the end). The script generates both
samples before drawing CV folds (fold seed 123), and ridge and Lasso
use the same folds. Neither $x$ nor $y$ is rescaled; centering
profiles out the unpenalized intercept (Exercise 1.12).

\textbf{(a) Ridge.}
For each penalty,
\[
\widehat b_\lambda
=
(\widetilde X\trans\widetilde X+n\lambda I)^{-1}
\widetilde X\trans\widetilde Y,
\qquad
\widehat\beta_{0,\lambda}
=
\overline Y-\overline X\trans\widehat b_\lambda,
\]
computed via an SVD of $\widetilde X$.
If $d_1,\ldots,d_r$ are the nonzero singular values
of $\widetilde X$, the effective degrees of freedom are
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{df}(\lambda)
=
1+\sum_{j=1}^r
\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+n\lambda},
}
\]
where the intercept contributes the leading 1.
The grid contains 240 positive penalties from
$10^4s$ to $10^{-8}s$, where $s=d_{\max}^2/n$.

\resultfigure{p20_ridge_path.pdf}{
Ridge coefficient path versus effective degrees of freedom.
The four predictors in the generating mean are highlighted; the
others are gray. Vertical lines mark the 10-fold CV choices.
}

\textbf{(b) Errors and cross-validation.}
Training and test errors are mean squared prediction errors:
\[
\operatorname{Err}_{\rm train}(\lambda)
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
(Y_i-\widehat Y_{i,\lambda})^2,
\qquad
\operatorname{Err}_{\rm test}(\lambda)
=
\frac1{n_{\rm test}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\rm test}}
(Y_i^{\rm test}-\widehat Y_{i,\lambda}^{\rm test})^2.
\]

For $K\in\{5,10\}$, let $e_k(\lambda)$ be the mean squared
held-out error in fold $k$, using a fit trained on the other folds.
The folds have equal size, so
\[
\operatorname{CV}_K(\lambda)
=
\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K e_k(\lambda),
\qquad
\operatorname{SE}_K(\lambda)
=
\frac{
\operatorname{sd}\{e_1(\lambda),\ldots,e_K(\lambda)\}
}{\sqrt K}.
\]

The minimum-CV choice is
$\lambda_{\min,K}\in\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{CV}_K(\lambda)$.
The one-standard-error choice is the largest penalty satisfying
\[
\operatorname{CV}_K(\lambda)
\leq
\operatorname{CV}_K(\lambda_{\min,K})
+
\operatorname{SE}_K(\lambda_{\min,K}),
\]
so the one-SE rule favors stronger regularization among fits whose
CV error is close to the minimum. This standard error is a
fold-based selection heuristic; overlapping training sets do not
produce independent fits.

CV is computed at common penalty values across folds. For plotting,
each penalty is mapped to the degrees of freedom (ridge) or
shrinkage fraction (Lasso) of its full-training-data fit.

\textbf{(c) Lasso.}
The objective is
\[
\frac1{2n}
\|Y-\beta_0\ones-Xb\|_2^2+\lambda\|b\|_1,
\]
fitted with \texttt{glmnet} (\texttt{alpha = 1},
\texttt{standardize = FALSE}, \texttt{intercept = TRUE}).
Define
\[
\lambda_{\max}
=
\max_j
\left|
\frac1n\widetilde X_j\trans\widetilde Y
\right|,
\]
the smallest penalty at which zero slopes satisfy the stationary
conditions. The grid has 240 log-spaced penalties from
$\lambda_{\max}$ to $10^{-4}\lambda_{\max}$.

For the full-data path and every CV training fit, the script checks
the original-scale KKT conditions, including the zero-mean-residual
condition for the intercept, and requires the maximum violation to be
below $10^{-6}\lambda_{\max}$. These checks are strong evidence that
the returned solutions are near-optimal, although small KKT residuals
do not by themselves certify that every coefficient is accurate to
the same tolerance.

The shrinkage fraction is
\[
f(\lambda)
=
\frac{\|\widehat b_\lambda\|_1}
{\|\widehat b_{\rm LS}\|_1}.
\]
For $p=20$, the centered design has full column rank almost
surely, so the least-squares slope reference is unique, and
$f(\lambda)\in[0,1]$ is nonincreasing in $\lambda$ (proved in
Exercise 1.18). Throughout, a slope is counted as active if
$|\widehat b_j|>10^{-8}$.

\textbf{Benchmark: the best linear predictor.}
Both methods fit linear models, so the natural target is the best
linear predictor $f^*(X)=\beta_0^*+X\trans b^*$ minimizing
$\E(Y-\beta_0-X\trans b)^2$, with $b^*=\Sigma^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}\{X,m(X)\}$.
Since $X\sim N(0,\Sigma)$ and $m$ is continuously differentiable,
Stein's lemma gives $\operatorname{Cov}\{X,m(X)\}=\Sigma\,\E\{\nabla m(X)\}$, so
\[
b^*=\E\{\nabla m(X)\}=(1,c,c,c,0,\ldots,0)\trans,
\qquad
c=\tfrac23\E(S_+)=\tfrac23\sqrt v\,\phi(0)\approx0.727394,
\]
where $S=X_2+X_3+X_4\sim N(0,v)$ with
$v=\Var(S)=3+2(0.8+0.8+0.64)=7.48$.
The slopes on $X_5,\ldots,X_p$ are exactly zero for every $p$, and
the intercept is $\beta_0^*=\E\,g(S)=v/6$, where $g(S)=S_+^2/3$.

The residual of this predictor is $g(S)-v/6-cS+\varepsilon$. Using
$\E S_+^4=3v^2/2$ and $\E S_+^3=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,v^{3/2}$, we get
$\Var\{g(S)\}=5v^2/36$ and $\operatorname{Cov}\{S,g(S)\}=cv$. Hence
\[
R^*_{\rm linear}
=0.25+\Var\{g(S)\}-c^2v
=0.25+v^2\Bigl(\frac5{36}-\frac2{9\pi}\Bigr)
\approx 4.0632.
\]
This is the minimum population prediction risk among affine
predictors (the \emph{linear floor}): noise variance $0.25$ plus
linear approximation error $3.8132$. (A Monte Carlo check of this
value is included in the script output.)
For any affine fitted predictor $\widehat f$ trained on data
$\mathcal D$, orthogonality of the best-linear-predictor residual to
affine functions gives
\[
R(\widehat f\mid\mathcal D)
=\E\{(Y-\widehat f(X))^2\mid\mathcal D\}
=R^*_{\rm linear}+\E\{(\widehat f(X)-f^*(X))^2\mid\mathcal D\},
\]
so regularization can affect only the second, excess-risk term.
If $\widehat f$ is selected using training data only, the test
sample is independent of $\mathcal D$, so
\[
\E\bigl[\widehat R_{\rm test}-R^*_{\rm linear}\mid\mathcal D\bigr]
=R(\widehat f\mid\mathcal D)-R^*_{\rm linear}.
\]
Thus test MSE minus the floor is an unbiased but noisy estimate of
the conditional excess risk; it can be negative even though the
excess risk itself cannot. This does not apply to the test-grid
oracle, whose penalty is chosen using the same test outcomes.

\textbf{Paired comparison.}

100-observation test MSE can be noisy. The table compares ridge and
Lasso on the same test points: for each rule it reports the mean of
$(Y_i-\widehat Y_i^{\rm ridge})^2-(Y_i-\widehat Y_i^{\rm lasso})^2$,

These standard errors describe test-sample uncertainty conditional

\textbf{(e) Findings.}
For $p=20$, every tuned fit has test MSE between 4.40 and 5.40,
against a linear floor of 4.06. Most of the population linear
prediction risk is approximation error (3.81 of 4.06), which
regularization cannot remove; regularization changes only the
excess risk above the best linear predictor. For the CV-selected
fits, the gap between test MSE and the floor estimates that excess
risk without bias, but with substantial noise from the 100-point
test sample.
Minimum-CV tuning came close to the test-grid benchmark: 10-fold
ridge selected the benchmark penalty exactly, and Lasso was within
0.17--0.22 of its benchmark. Ridge was lower than Lasso under every
matching rule, by 0.13--0.37, but the paired standard errors are
0.13--0.28 ($|z|\le1.8$), so the paired differences provide limited
evidence of a difference in conditional prediction risk on this
realization. The one-SE rule chose penalties about
4--10 times larger and raised test MSE by 0.44--0.70 for both
methods. The Lasso test-grid benchmark is close to the
least-squares end of its path ($f\approx0.95$), whereas the ridge
benchmark retains about 14.2 effective degrees of freedom out of a
maximum of 21; stronger one-SE regularization worsened observed
prediction performance for both methods in this realization. The
one-SE Lasso fits keep only 3 slopes and drop $X_2$.

\end{solution}

\textbf{Rank and reference convention.}
At $p=200$, centering $n=200$ observations gives
\[
\operatorname{rank}(\widetilde X)\leq199.
\]
The unpenalized least-squares slope solution is therefore
nonunique. The script uses the minimum-Euclidean-norm solution
\[
\widehat b_{\rm LS}
=
\widetilde X^+\widetilde Y
\]
as the explicit reference for the Lasso shrinkage fraction.

Different least-squares slope solutions can have different
$L_1$ norms, so this convention affects the numerical fraction.
It cannot, however, push the fraction above 1.
Let $L(b)=\frac1{2n}\|\widetilde Y-\widetilde Xb\|_2^2$ and let
$b_{\rm LS}$ be \emph{any} least-squares solution. For $\lambda>0$,
optimality of $\widehat b_\lambda$ and of $b_{\rm LS}$ give
\[
L(\widehat b_\lambda)+\lambda\|\widehat b_\lambda\|_1
\le L(b_{\rm LS})+\lambda\|b_{\rm LS}\|_1,
\qquad
L(b_{\rm LS})\le L(\widehat b_\lambda),
\]
so $\|\widehat b_\lambda\|_1\le\|b_{\rm LS}\|_1$ and
$f(\lambda)\in[0,1]$ whenever $\|b_{\rm LS}\|_1>0$.

The fraction is also monotone. For $0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2$, write
$P_i=\|\widehat b_{\lambda_i}\|_1$. Optimality at each penalty gives
\[
L(\widehat b_{\lambda_1})+\lambda_1P_1\le L(\widehat b_{\lambda_2})+\lambda_1P_2,
\qquad
L(\widehat b_{\lambda_2})+\lambda_2P_2\le L(\widehat b_{\lambda_1})+\lambda_2P_1.
\]
Adding yields $(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)(P_2-P_1)\le0$, so $P_2\le P_1$:
$f$ is nonincreasing in $\lambda$. Individual coefficients need not
be monotone, but the total $L_1$ norm is. The script checks both
properties; no violation occurred.

\textbf{Small-penalty limit.}
At $p=200$ the fraction reaches only about 0.31 at the end of the
grid, unlike $p=20$ and $p=100$, where it approaches 1. To see why,
let $\mathcal B$ be the set of least-squares solutions. The bound
above gives $\|\widehat b_\lambda\|_1\le\min_{b\in\mathcal B}\|b\|_1$,
so the path is bounded and has accumulation points as
$\lambda\downarrow0$. Moreover
$L(\widehat b_\lambda)\le\min L+\lambda\|b_{\rm LS}\|_1\to\min L$.
Hence every accumulation point $\bar b$
\begin{itemize}
\item minimizes the squared loss, i.e.\ $\bar b\in\mathcal B$, and
\item has minimum $L_1$ norm within $\mathcal B$.
\end{itemize}
If the minimum-$L_1$ least-squares solution is unique (as it is
for a design in general position), then $\widehat b_\lambda$
converges to it; without uniqueness, only the accumulation-point
statement holds. The minimum-$L_2$ reference $\widetilde X^+\widetilde Y$
need not minimize the $L_1$ norm over $\mathcal B$, so the limiting
fraction can be strictly less than 1. The value 0.31 is computed
at the positive endpoint penalty $10^{-4}\lambda_{\max}$, so it is
not itself an estimate of the exact limiting fraction.

At $p=n=200$, very weak ridge penalties approach interpolation of
the training data, so the training error approaches zero while the
test error becomes very large. The ridge coefficient-path figure
therefore adds a detail panel that excludes the
near-interpolation penalties.
Positive ridge penalties remain well defined even within CV
folds whose training sample size is smaller than the number
of covariates.

Changing $p$ changes the realized observations, even when
the seed is the same. Thus comparisons across dimensions are not
paired comparisons on identical responses, although the generating
mean formula remains unchanged.

\textbf{Findings for $p=100$.}
The methods had similar test-grid minima (ridge 4.54, Lasso 4.44),
and minimum-CV tuning slightly favored ridge on the observed test

\textbf{Findings for $p=200$.}
Minimum-CV Lasso achieved test MSEs of 4.02--4.05 while keeping
10--13 nonzero slopes including all of $X_1,\ldots,X_4$; ridge
achieved 5.82--5.91. The Lasso values lie slightly below the linear
